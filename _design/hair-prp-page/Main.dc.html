The basics, plainly

What Is Hair PRP Treatment?

PRP stands for platelet-rich plasma. A small volume of blood is drawn from your arm, exactly as it would be for a routine blood test, and spun in a centrifuge. Spinning separates the blood into layers, and we keep the plasma fraction in which the platelets are concentrated.

That concentrate is injected into the scalp at the depth where hair follicles sit, across the areas that are thinning. Platelets carry growth factors — signalling proteins the body normally uses for repair. The working principle in hair loss is that delivering them around miniaturising follicles helps hold those follicles in their growth phase for longer and improves the blood supply feeding them.

Two things follow from that, and both matter more than any marketing claim. First, PRP acts on follicles that are still alive but shrinking; where a follicle is gone — a fully bald, shiny scalp, or scarred skin — there is nothing left for it to act on. Second, it does not switch off the process driving the hair loss, so a result is maintained rather than banked.

It is also honest to say that the published evidence for PRP in pattern hair loss is supportive but uneven, largely because protocols differ so much between clinics: how the blood is spun, how concentrated the result is, how much is injected and how often. We treat PRP as one part of a plan, not the whole plan.

Suitability

Who May Consider Hair PRP?

PRP is at its most useful early, while follicles are miniaturising rather than lost. These are the presentations we most often treat with it — suitability is still confirmed only after examination.

Early pattern hair loss

Male or female pattern loss caught in its early or moderate stages, where hair is getting finer rather than absent.

Diffuse thinning in women

Widening parting and reduced volume, once thyroid, iron, vitamin D and hormonal causes have been checked and addressed.

Increased shedding

Noticeably more hair on the pillow, in the shower or on the comb, where the examination points to follicles under strain rather than lost.

Alongside a transplant

Used to support the native hair around a transplanted area, which continues to thin on its own timetable after surgery.

When medication is not an option

Patients who cannot tolerate, or choose not to take, standard oral or topical hair-loss medication, and want a non-surgical route.

Not ready for surgery

Younger patients whose pattern has not stabilised, where holding what exists is the sensible move before any transplant is planned.

Straight comparison

PRP, GFC, Mesotherapy and Medication — What Is Actually Different

Clinics in Gurgaon use these names loosely and sometimes interchangeably, which makes quotes impossible to compare. Here is what each one is, in plain terms.

Treatment What is injected or used Typically used for Worth knowing
Hair PRP Your own platelet-rich plasma, spun from your blood on the day. Early to moderate pattern loss, diffuse thinning, support around a transplant. Autologous, so no donor material. Results depend heavily on the preparation protocol and need maintenance.
GFC Growth factor concentrate — platelets are broken down so the growth factors are released, then the cells are removed. The same indications as PRP; marketed as a more refined version of it. Also made from your blood, using a kit. The published evidence base is smaller and newer than for PRP.
Mesotherapy A manufactured cocktail of vitamins, peptides or medication — not made from your blood. Scalp nourishment protocols, often sold bundled with other treatments. Formulations vary widely between clinics, so ask exactly what is in the syringe.
Medical therapy Prescribed topical or oral hair-loss medication, taken daily at home. The long-term backbone of treating pattern hair loss at any stage. Best-established evidence of the four. PRP usually runs alongside it rather than instead of it.
Hair transplant Your own follicles, moved surgically from the donor area into thinning zones. Areas where follicles are already lost and no injection can bring them back. A different category of treatment. Read about hair transplant in Gurgaon.

If a clinic quotes you for “PRP” without saying how many sessions, how much blood is drawn or which preparation is used, you are not comparing like with like. Those are fair questions to ask anywhere, including here.

Inside the session

How Does a PRP Session Work?

Most of the session is preparation. The injecting part is the short bit at the end.

1

Check-in and review

Your scalp is looked at again, any change since the last session is noted, and the treatment zones for the day are marked.

2

Blood draw

A small volume is taken from a vein in the arm into a prepared tube. It feels the same as giving blood for a routine test and takes a couple of minutes.

3

Centrifugation

The tube is spun so the blood separates into layers. The platelet-rich plasma is drawn off into a syringe. This is the part of the session that takes the longest.

4

Scalp preparation

The area is cleaned, and topical anaesthetic or cooling is used to take the edge off the injections. Nobody is asked to just tolerate it.

5

Injection

A series of small injections is placed across the marked zones with a fine needle, spaced to cover the thinning area evenly at follicle depth. This part usually takes ten to fifteen minutes.

6

Aftercare and next date

You are given washing, exercise and sun instructions, and the next session is booked before you leave. You go home the same day and drive yourself if you came by car.

On the day

  • Eat and drink normally before you come — blood is being drawn.
  • Come with clean, dry hair and no styling product.
  • Tell the doctor about blood thinners and anti-inflammatory painkillers.
  • Leave the scalp alone that evening; wash as instructed the next day.
  • Skip the gym, sauna, steam and alcohol for about 24 hours.

Sessions & timeline

How Many Sessions, and When Results Show

A general guide to the pattern most patients follow. Your own schedule is set by your doctor after the assessment, and adjusted at your reviews.

Weeks 0–12

The initial course

Sessions spaced about four weeks apart. Most plans run to three or four sessions in this phase, sometimes more for a larger area.

Weeks 6–10

Shedding settles first

The first change people usually report is less hair coming out, before anything looks visibly thicker. This is a reasonable early sign, not the result itself.

Months 3–6

Texture and density

Where there is a response, hair tends to feel coarser and the parting looks less open. Judged against your baseline photographs, not against how it feels on a good day.

Ongoing

Maintenance

Top-up sessions are commonly spaced every three to six months. Stop entirely and the underlying hair loss carries on from where it left off.

Not everyone responds to PRP, and response cannot be predicted from a photograph before treatment starts. If your review shows little or no change, the honest answer is to change the plan — not to sell you a longer course. Timelines here are general patient education rather than a promise of results.

Safety

Side Effects and Who Should Not Have PRP

Because the injected material comes from your own blood, a reaction to the material itself is not expected. The effects that do occur are mostly from the needles and settle quickly.

Commonly reported after a session

  • Tenderness and a tight, bruised feeling in the scalp for a day or two
  • Redness and pinpoint marks at the injection sites
  • Mild swelling, occasionally settling towards the forehead overnight
  • Headache or a heavy-headed feeling on the day
  • Itching or transient tingling as the scalp settles
  • Bruising at the arm where blood was drawn

Infection at injection sites is uncommon but possible with any injection, which is why sterile technique and aftercare instructions matter. Contact the clinic if you develop spreading redness, pus or fever.

When PRP is not offered, or is deferred

This is not a complete list. It is what most often changes the plan at assessment.

  • Active infection, dermatitis or open skin over the area to be injected
  • Low platelet counts, platelet function disorders or bleeding disorders
  • Ongoing anticoagulant treatment, until reviewed with the prescribing doctor
  • Blood cancers and some other active malignancies
  • Poorly controlled systemic illness, or an acute unwell episode
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding, where treatment is usually deferred
  • Areas that are fully bald or scarred, where no viable follicle remains

Bring your prescriptions and supplements to the consultation. Blood thinners and regular anti-inflammatory painkillers are the two most commonly missed items, and both matter here.

FAQs

Frequently Asked Questions

Answers are general patient education. Anything specific to your case is answered at the assessment.

Ask on WhatsApp
What is hair PRP treatment?+

A treatment in which a small volume of your own blood is spun in a centrifuge to concentrate the platelets, and that platelet-rich plasma is injected into the thinning areas of your scalp. The aim is to support follicles that are miniaturising, using growth factors your own platelets carry.

Does PRP actually work for hair loss?+

For the right case — early to moderate pattern loss with follicles still present — there is reasonable evidence that PRP can reduce shedding and improve density. The evidence is not uniform, because protocols differ so much between clinics, and a proportion of patients do not respond. It works best as part of a plan alongside medical treatment, not as a substitute for one.

How many PRP sessions will I need?+

Most initial courses are planned as three or four sessions about four weeks apart, sometimes more where a larger area is being treated. Maintenance sessions are then commonly spaced every three to six months. Your own number is set after the assessment and confirmed in writing before the first session.

Is PRP painful?+

It is uncomfortable rather than painful for most people. The blood draw feels like any blood test; the scalp injections sting briefly, and topical anaesthetic or cooling is used to reduce that. The scalp usually feels tender for a day or so afterwards. Pain tolerance varies.

How long does one session take?+

Roughly 30 to 60 minutes from arrival to leaving. Most of that is the blood draw, the spin and preparing the scalp; the injections themselves usually take ten to fifteen minutes. It is a walk-in, walk-out appointment.

When will I see results from PRP?+

Reduced shedding is usually the first change people notice, often around six to ten weeks. Changes in texture and density, where they occur, are generally assessed from three to six months against your baseline photographs. Nothing meaningful can be judged after a single session.

Is PRP permanent, or do I need to keep going?+

It is not permanent. PRP does not stop the underlying process causing pattern hair loss, so a result is held with maintenance sessions and, in most cases, ongoing medical treatment. If you stop everything, thinning resumes from where it had reached.

What is the difference between PRP and GFC?+

Both start from your own blood. PRP injects the platelet-rich plasma itself. GFC breaks the platelets down first so the growth factors are released, then removes the cells, leaving an acellular concentrate. GFC is marketed as a refinement of PRP; its published evidence base is smaller and newer. Neither is automatically the better choice for a given scalp.

Can PRP regrow hair on a completely bald area?+

No. PRP acts on follicles that still exist. Where the scalp is fully bald and smooth, or scarred, the follicles are gone and there is nothing for the treatment to support. Those areas are a hair transplant question, not a PRP question.

Is PRP safe? What are the side effects?+

Because the injected material is made from your own blood, a reaction to the material itself is not expected. The usual effects come from the needles: tenderness, redness, pinpoint marks, mild swelling, occasional headache and bruising at the arm. Infection at an injection site is uncommon but possible, which is why sterile technique and aftercare instructions matter.

Who should not have PRP?+

Treatment is usually not offered, or is deferred, with active infection or dermatitis over the injection area, low platelet counts or bleeding disorders, ongoing anticoagulant treatment until reviewed, blood cancers and some other active malignancies, poorly controlled systemic illness, and in pregnancy and breastfeeding. This is not a complete list — suitability is decided at the consultation.

Can PRP be done after a hair transplant?+

It is commonly used to support the native hair around a transplanted area, which keeps thinning on its own timetable after surgery. Timing relative to the procedure is decided by the treating doctor, since the scalp needs to have healed first.

Can women have PRP for hair loss?+

Yes, and female pattern thinning is one of the more common reasons patients come for it. The order matters, though: thyroid function, iron stores, vitamin D and hormonal causes are checked and treated first, because a proportion of female shedding improves once those are corrected without any injections at all.

Do I need to stop my hair-loss medication during PRP?+

Usually not — prescribed medical therapy generally continues alongside a PRP course, because the two do different jobs. What does need reviewing before a session is anything affecting clotting, including blood thinners and regular anti-inflammatory painkillers. Bring your full medication list.

How much does hair PRP treatment cost in Gurgaon?+

Advertised prices in Gurgaon range widely, because a session is not a standard unit — the volume drawn, the preparation method, the area treated and the number of sessions included all differ. Compare on the course, not the session, and ask what happens if you do not respond.
